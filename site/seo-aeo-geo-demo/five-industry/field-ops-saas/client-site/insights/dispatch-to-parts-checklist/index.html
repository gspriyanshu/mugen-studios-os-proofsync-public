The short answer

What must happen between dispatch and parts control?

The job needs a defined parts requirement, source, reservation, picker or technician owner, issue confirmation, substitution rule, consumption or return event and an exception path that both dispatch and inventory can see.

What this cannot decide

The checklist must be adapted to the real team's roles, systems and accounting rules.

01

Confirm job readiness before assignment

The handoff starts with a complete service requirement: site, asset, task, skills, time constraints and expected parts. A dispatcher should not infer the parts plan from a short free-text symptom when diagnosis or asset history can narrow the requirement.

Readiness should show full, partial or unknown part coverage. Unknown is a useful state because it prevents a job with unresolved diagnosis from appearing equivalent to one with a verified reservation.

02

Make custody acknowledgement explicit

When branch stock is issued to a technician, both the source and destination need the same transfer identity. Scanning or acknowledgement confirms custody; merely printing a pick list does not prove the parts reached the vehicle.

Substitutions should preserve the requested item and the supplied item. That history helps reviewers distinguish a valid field decision from unexplained catalogue drift.

03

Reconcile at job close without blocking honest exceptions

The close flow should ask which issued parts were consumed, returned, retained or damaged. It should allow an exception with a reason and follow-up owner instead of encouraging technicians to choose an inaccurate value just to finish the job.

Dispatch, inventory and service history should then reflect the same outcome. Any mismatch belongs in a visible queue with age and ownership, not in an unmonitored comment field.

The short answer

Why should reserved and consumed parts be separate events?

Reservation protects planned stock; allocation links a specific source or quantity to the job; consumption confirms actual use. Keeping them separate shows what is promised, picked, installed, returned or still unresolved.

What this cannot decide

Terminology varies by platform; a real implementation needs an approved data dictionary.

01

Reservation protects intent

A reservation records that a quantity is expected to support a specific work order or appointment. Physical custody may not have changed, but other planners should see that the quantity is no longer freely promiseable.

The record needs a source location, destination job, quantity, creator and release condition. Without an expiry or cancellation path, old reservations quietly reduce available stock and make purchasing signals unreliable.

02

Allocation chooses the source and fulfils the promise

Allocation turns a general requirement into a sourcing decision: these units, from this branch or vehicle, will fulfil the job. Some systems combine reservation and allocation, but operations still need to know when a promise became tied to a physical source.

Partial allocation must stay visible. If three units are required and only two are sourced, the work order should carry a one-unit shortage rather than appearing fully ready because an allocation record exists.

03

Consumption records what the job actually used

Consumption is evidence of actual use, not a synonym for loading a truck. It should reference the work order, part, quantity, technician, time and—when relevant—the serviced asset or serial number.

Unused allocated stock needs a return, transfer or retained-on-van decision. Closing the job without resolving the remaining quantity breaks the chain between the work record and the next physical count.

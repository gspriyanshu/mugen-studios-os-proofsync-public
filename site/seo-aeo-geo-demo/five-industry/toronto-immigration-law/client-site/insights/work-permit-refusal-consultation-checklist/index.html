The short answer

What records should someone gather after a work-permit refusal?

Keep the refusal letter, complete application and forms, every uploaded document, payment and submission confirmations, correspondence, current status information and a dated timeline. Share them securely with an authorized professional for individual advice.

What this cannot decide

The checklist does not diagnose the refusal, calculate time limits or choose a remedy.

01

Preserve the exact refusal package

Keep the refusal letter, officer decision notes if supplied, the complete application as filed, document checklist, payment record and all later IRCC correspondence. IRCC now provides decision notes with many work-permit and extension refusals, subject to its current exclusions and redactions. These records should remain in their original form.

Create a simple index rather than rewriting the officer's reasons from memory. Record the document name, date received, page count and storage location. The index helps a licensed adviser see the same record that produced the decision; it does not interpret the reasons or show that an error occurred.

02

Build a factual timeline without filling gaps

List the application date, biometrics or information requests, responses, permit or status dates, refusal date and the date the decision was received. Use documents or account records for each entry and mark unsupported dates as unknown. Do not estimate a date merely to make the chronology look complete.

Status, restoration, work authorization and court timing questions can carry serious consequences. A general checklist should flag those questions for immediate review but must not tell a person that they may work, remain in Canada, restore status or use a particular deadline.

03

Separate reapplication questions from court-review questions

IRCC's Help Centre says there is no formal appeal for temporary-residence application decisions and describes reapplication and Federal Court judicial review at a high level. The Federal Court explains that immigration judicial review begins with an application for leave and follows its own rules and time limits. These are distinct routes.

For a consultation, prepare questions rather than conclusions: what reason was recorded, whether material facts changed, what new evidence exists, whether a legal or fairness issue is alleged and what timing applies to the actual decision. Only an authorized professional reviewing the record can advise on strategy; no route guarantees a new or favourable result.
